The Salt Roads

How a medieval trade in preservation quietly drew the map of modern Europe.

By Ada Merriwether

Before refrigeration, salt was not a seasoning but an infrastructure. A city that could not preserve its autumn slaughter did not survive its winter, and so the roads that carried salt inland became the arteries along which everything else eventually moved: grain, coin, soldiers, and finally ideas.

The Via Salaria ran from the Adriatic marshes to Rome, and it was old before Rome was. Herodotus describes caravans crossing the Sahara for it. In the Alps, whole principalities were financed by a single brine spring, taxed at the wellhead and again at every bridge, until the price of a barrel at market bore almost no relation to the cost of making it.

The salt tax and its discontents

What made salt such a reliable instrument of state was precisely what made it hated. Everyone needs it, nobody can make it at home, and it travels badly without official roads. The French gabelle, levied unevenly across provinces, obliged households to buy a fixed quantity each year whether they wanted it or not.

The people will suffer much, but they will not suffer to be told what they must buy.

Smuggling followed, as it always does. Contraband salt crossed provincial borders in false-bottomed carts and inside the linings of coats, and the customs service that grew up to stop it became one of the largest arms of the pre-revolutionary state.

  • Brine springs were taxed at the wellhead.
  • Roads were tolled by the barrel.
  • Households were assessed a compulsory annual quantity.

By the time the tax was abolished the roads it paid for had outlived their purpose, and the salt itself had become the cheapest thing in the kitchen. What remained were the routes, and the towns that had grown up where the carts stopped for the night, which is most of them.
