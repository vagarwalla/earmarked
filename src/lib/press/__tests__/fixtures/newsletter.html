The Longest Winter of the Little Ice Age

By way of introduction: this week we are in the 1690s, which were, by any measure anyone kept, the worst decade of weather in the recorded history of northern Europe. The harvest failed seven years running in Scotland, and roughly one person in seven did not survive it.

What makes the period legible to us now is that it was cold enough, for long enough, that people wrote it down. Parish registers, grain price ledgers, and the diaries of a handful of obsessive gentlemen give us something close to a continuous record, and the record says the Thames froze hard enough to hold a fair on it.

The frost continues; the river a solid highway, with booths, and printing presses upon the ice.

The mechanism was probably volcanic — a run of large eruptions loading the stratosphere with sulphate aerosol — layered on top of an already cool baseline from reduced solar activity. But the human consequences were not distributed by mechanism. They were distributed by whether your country had grain reserves, functioning ports, and a government that believed famine was its problem.
